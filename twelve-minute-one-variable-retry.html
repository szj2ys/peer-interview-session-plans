Plan 5 of 5 · Two people; one speaker and one listener for this session

12-minute one-variable retry with two takes

Repeat one answer with a single planned change, then compare what the listener actually understood.

Run the timetableUse the session sheetGive bounded feedback

When to use this format

Use this short session after you already have an answer worth refining. The task is to test one revision, not to produce a polished performance by changing everything at once. The speaker gives two takes of the same answer to the same question. Between them, the pair chooses one change, such as moving the decision constraint to the opening. Keep the underlying facts, the listener, the question, and the two-minute answer limit stable. This is a practical comparison for choosing the next rehearsal; it is not a controlled experiment, a hiring test, or proof that one technique caused an improvement.

Roles, materials, and the opening cue

The speaker supplies a true, non-confidential experience and chooses what a listener should understand. The listener reads the same question for both takes, runs the timer, and writes a brief account of what they understood before discussing the answer. The listener does not coach during either take. Put two columns labelled A and B on paper, plus a separate line for the planned change. Recording is optional and unnecessary; if used, agree on it before the session and keep both takes under the same conditions. There is only one speaker in this twelve-minute plan. Reversing roles requires another complete session.

The opening cue is: “We will keep the facts and question the same. After the first take, we will choose one thing to change and check whether it helps you understand the point.” Write the exact question and a concrete listener task, such as “Name the decision and the constraint behind it.” Avoid a broad target like “sound better,” which invites many simultaneous changes. If the speaker has no prepared incident, spend another session choosing one; do not consume this plan by inventing a history. Do not rehearse a fictional example as though it happened to you.

The complete timetable

Time check: 1 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 12 minutes. Intervals use a running session clock, including transitions and debrief.

  1. 0–1 minutes · Fix the question and listener task

    Write the exact question, the true incident, and the understanding to check. Agree that the listener will not interrupt either take.

  2. 1–3 minutes · Take A

    Listener reads the question; speaker answers within the two-minute block. Listener notes the answer’s meaning rather than suggesting improvements.

  3. 3–4 minutes · Capture the first understanding

    Listener writes and reads a short retelling. Speaker corrects factual mishearing but does not yet rewrite the answer.

  4. 4–6 minutes · Choose one variable

    Select one revision tied to the observed difficulty. Write what will change and what will stay the same. If no difficulty appeared, choose a narrower test or keep the answer as it is.

  5. 6–8 minutes · Take B

    Listener reads the identical question. Speaker gives the answer again with the planned revision and the same facts and time limit.

  6. 8–10 minutes · Compare the two understandings

    Listener writes a second retelling before rereading the first. Compare the agreed task, note any extra changes, and identify what remains unclear.

  7. 10–12 minutes · Debrief and retain a decision

    Speaker chooses to keep, reject, or retest the revision. Record a reason grounded in this comparison and one next practice action.

A small fictional example

Fictional example: a candidate explains choosing a simple appointment form for a volunteer clinic. Take A begins with the history of the project and mentions limited volunteer setup time near the end. The listener remembers the form but misses the constraint. The single planned change is ordering: Take B opens with the setup-time constraint, then gives the same choice and facts. The listener can now name the constraint. The pair keeps the ordering for another rehearsal but acknowledges that hearing the story twice may also have helped. They do not add a new outcome statistic or declare the second take objectively superior.

Feedback prompts and a useful example

The listener answers the same questions for both takes: “What decision did I hear?”, “What reason or constraint did I understand?”, and “Which part of that account is uncertain?” Useful bounded feedback is: “In A, my retelling named the short form but no constraint. In B, I wrote that volunteers had limited setup time. You moved that fact to the opening. I would keep that order for another listener and check whether they also understand it.” This says what changed in the listener’s record without claiming causation. If the goal is different, replace these prompts before Take A rather than choosing a favourable measure afterward.

If the session goes off plan

If Take B changes the facts, adds a metric, and changes the order, mark the comparison as mixed rather than attributing the result to one variable. Use the remaining time to identify which change to isolate next; do not squeeze in an unplanned third full take. If the listener interrupts A but not B, note the unequal conditions. If the second answer is shorter only because the listener already knows the context, ask whether a new listener would have enough information. If either take runs out of time, mark the missing ending and keep the same limit. If the first take was already clear, an unchanged answer or an inconclusive comparison is a legitimate outcome.

Debrief and constraints

End with a decision, not a score. “Keep for a new listener,” “reject because it hid the team’s contribution,” and “retest because two things changed” are all usable results. The speaker chooses whether the listener’s proposed wording feels accurate and natural. Note the limits: the listener knows the hypothesis, the second take benefits from practice, and one person’s comprehension may not represent another person’s. For a later session, try the retained version with a new listener, while still preserving the true facts. This plan helps organise a small revision and its evidence; it cannot establish a general communication skill from two attempts.

Printable session sheet

Use paper, or type short notes into these temporary boxes and use your browser’s Print command. This page sends and stores no answers. Reloading or closing may discard typed notes. Keep notes inside the visible boxes when printing; copy anything you want to retain first. Mark uncertain wording as a paraphrase.
