Plan 2 of 5 · Three people; everyone takes all three roles

30-minute triad with an observer and role rotation

Separate interviewing from observation, then rotate through three complete eight-minute rounds.

Run the timetableUse the session sheetGive bounded feedback

When to use this format

Use a triad when an answer needs closer observation than a busy interviewer can provide. The third person listens for a single agreed communication feature and records evidence while the interviewer keeps the conversation moving. Rotation matters: nobody remains the permanent critic, and everyone experiences how hard it is to ask a useful follow-up. This session produces three short rehearsals with equal role coverage. It is not a panel interview and does not combine three people’s opinions into a hiring verdict. Each candidate chooses an ordinary real experience they are authorised to discuss.

Roles, materials, and the opening cue

Name the participants A, B, and C. Round one has A as candidate, B as interviewer, and C as observer. Round two has B as candidate, C as interviewer, and A as observer. Round three has C as candidate, A as interviewer, and B as observer. Write this order where everyone can see it; do not rely on remembering who moves next. The observer owns the timer and notes, but does not join the questioning. The candidate chooses a focus such as “Can the listener distinguish my decision from the team’s decision?” The interviewer asks one opening question and at most one follow-up during the answer block.

Place three sheets on the table, one per future candidate, and agree that notes belong to that candidate at the end. The opening facilitator says: “Observe the chosen focus only. Record something you heard before offering an interpretation. No scores, diagnoses, or guesses about employability.” Each candidate writes their opening question and focus in the first three minutes. Set a visible timer for the shared round. Recording is unnecessary; a short note labelled as exact wording or paraphrase is enough. Anyone may replace a confidential detail with a role label or decline a question without losing their next turn.

The complete timetable

Time check: 3 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 3 = 30 minutes. Intervals use a running session clock, including transitions and debrief.

  1. 0–3 minutes · Set the rotation and observation focus

    A reads the role order. Everyone writes a question and one observable focus. Confirm that the observer will remain silent until feedback.

  2. 3–11 minutes · Round one: A / B / C

    A is candidate, B interviews, C observes and times. Follow the eight-minute internal schedule below, ending with the handoff.

  3. 11–19 minutes · Round two: B / C / A

    B is candidate, C interviews, A observes and times. Move the candidate’s sheet with the candidate, not with the observer.

  4. 19–27 minutes · Round three: C / A / B

    C is candidate, A interviews, B observes and times. Use the same limits even if the group has become more comfortable.

  5. 27–30 minutes · Compare lessons about the roles

    Each person gets one minute to name an answer revision and one interviewing or observing habit they want to change.

Inside each eight-minute round

Minute 0–1: the candidate states the focus and the interviewer reads the question. Minutes 1–4: the candidate answers and the interviewer may ask one follow-up; both fit inside these three minutes. Minutes 4–6: the observer gives one minute of observation, then the candidate has one minute to correct the record or ask what the observer meant. Minute 6–7: the candidate retries one short passage. Minute 7–8: the interviewer says whether the retry addressed the original question, and the group hands over the next sheet. The internal budget is 1 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 8 minutes. The observer says “feedback” at minute four and “handoff” at minute seven; these cues prevent a long interview from consuming the replay.

A small fictional example

Fictional example: B discusses a scheduling conflict between two student clubs. B’s focus is whether the listener can understand the decision rule. Observer A writes a paraphrase: “You moved one meeting, but the reason appeared only after the follow-up.” A asks B to confirm that account. B explains the available-room constraint, then retries the opening with that constraint first. Interviewer C can now explain why the move was made. The observation supports that local revision; it does not establish that B generally makes good decisions.

Feedback prompts and a useful example

The observer uses this order: “The focus was…”, “I heard or noted…”, “I understood…”, and “One test for the retry is…”. Useful feedback for the fictional round is: “Your focus was the decision rule. My note is a paraphrase: the room limit appeared after the follow-up. In the retry, state the room limit before naming the chosen meeting time, and let the interviewer tell us whether the reason is clear.” Ask the interviewer separately, “Did the answer address your actual question?” That answer checks relevance; it is not a second general critique. Avoid two people interrupting the candidate with competing rewrites.

If the session goes off plan

If the observer cannot remember a phrase, admit the gap and describe only their understanding. Do not reconstruct a polished quote from memory. If the observer joins the questioning, the timer owner says, “Keep that for the observation turn,” and returns the floor to the interviewer. If a round runs long, cut its optional follow-up or shorten its retry; never remove the next candidate’s round. If someone arrives late, pause the three-person format and use a pair rehearsal until all roles are filled. An absent observer cannot be simulated by asking the interviewer to write a complete transcript while speaking.

Debrief and constraints

At minute twenty-seven, stop practising even if one answer remains imperfect. Each participant says what changed when they were responsible for observing rather than asking. Check that every person has one candidate round, one interviewer round, and one observer round marked on their sheet. The candidate may disagree with a proposed rewrite; record the disagreement instead of forcing consensus. Return notes to their subject and decide whether to keep or discard them. Treat the observer’s account as a limited record of one short attempt, especially when there was no recording or when the topic was unfamiliar.

Printable session sheet

Use paper, or type short notes into these temporary boxes and use your browser’s Print command. This page sends and stores no answers. Reloading or closing may discard typed notes. Keep notes inside the visible boxes when printing; copy anything you want to retain first. Mark uncertain wording as a paraphrase.
