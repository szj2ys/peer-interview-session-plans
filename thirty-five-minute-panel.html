Plan 3 of 5 · Four people; one candidate, one chair, two question owners

35-minute panel rehearsal with bounded question ownership

Practise switching between listeners while preventing interruptions, repeated questions, and an unlimited question queue.

Run the timetableUse the session sheetGive bounded feedback

When to use this format

Use this format when a candidate wants to practise answering several people in one conversation. The learning task is managing attention and connecting answers across perspectives, not surviving aggressive questioning. A chair gives each question owner a limited topic and speaking block, so the candidate knows whom to answer and the panel does not become a pile-on. Only one person is the candidate in these thirty-five minutes. Schedule another full session to rotate that role; do not advertise this plan as equal candidate practice for all four participants.

Roles, materials, and the opening cue

The candidate supplies a true, non-confidential project and names one communication goal. The chair manages time, introduces speakers, holds deferred questions, and records a brief topic map. Question owner A owns one technical or task decision. Question owner B owns collaboration or coordination around the same experience. Each owner prepares one opening question and may ask one follow-up in their block. They may not answer for the candidate or take over the other owner’s subject. The chair’s clarification block may connect an unresolved point from those two topics but may not introduce a third assessment area. Everyone needs paper; one visible timer is enough.

The chair opens: “This is a rehearsal, not a selection meeting. We will use one topic per question owner, one voice at a time, and a clear handoff. You may ask who a question is addressed to, request clarification, or decline private details.” Write A’s topic and B’s topic before the timer reaches minute four. A workable pair is “why you chose the implementation approach” and “how you coordinated the people affected.” If both owners choose the same question, one changes it now. Agree on the stop cue “handoff,” and leave recordings off unless a separate explicit agreement has already been made.

The complete timetable

Time check: 4 + 3 + 6 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 3 = 35 minutes. Intervals use a running session clock, including transitions and debrief.

  1. 0–4 minutes · Brief and allocate topics

    Chair names the goal, boundaries, question owners, and stop cue. Candidate approves the experience to discuss. Owners resolve overlapping questions now.

  2. 4–7 minutes · Candidate opening

    Chair asks for a short account of the experience. Candidate introduces enough context for both owners, without trying to answer every possible question.

  3. 7–13 minutes · Owner A’s block

    Chair says, “A, your question about the decision.” A asks one question and at most one follow-up. Candidate may clarify the question before answering.

  4. 13–19 minutes · Owner B’s block

    Chair says, “B, your question about coordination.” B avoids repeating A’s question. Candidate connects the collaboration detail to the same experience.

  5. 19–22 minutes · Chair’s bounded clarification

    Chair chooses one unresolved link between the two topics. If none remains, candidate briefly summarises the decision and coordination; no surprise topic is added.

  6. 22–25 minutes · Candidate questions

    Candidate asks the panel about its understanding or a practice concern. Owners answer as peers, not as representatives of a real employer.

  7. 25–28 minutes · Silent feedback preparation

    All three panel members write before anyone speaks. Each separates an observation, an interpretation, and one possible revision.

  8. 28–32 minutes · Feedback handoff

    Chair gives each owner one minute for one observation, then the candidate has two minutes to respond or clarify. Chair prevents a new interview question from entering this block.

  9. 32–35 minutes · Debrief and close

    Candidate identifies one useful next rehearsal. Chair checks interruptions, duplicate questions, and handoffs. Agree whether to schedule a separate role rotation.

A small fictional example

Fictional example: a candidate describes introducing a new booking form for a community workshop. Owner A asks why a short form was chosen over a more detailed one. Owner B asks how volunteer hosts were prepared for missing information. B notices that A already asked about required fields, so asks about the handoff instructions instead of repeating the form-design question. The chair later asks how the chosen form and host instructions worked together. That bridge stays within the allocated topics. Nobody invents a conversion-rate result or presents the exercise as a test of hiring suitability.

Feedback prompts and a useful example

Before speaking, each owner answers: “Which sentence let me understand my topic?”, “What did I infer that the candidate did not explicitly establish?”, and “What single change would make the handoff to another listener easier?” Bounded useful feedback sounds like: “When B began, you restarted the whole project history. I already understood the event context from your opening. Try one linking sentence that names the host handoff, then answer B’s question directly.” The candidate can test that suggestion later without accepting a claim about their personality. The chair gives process feedback separately: “We interrupted once during the clarification; next time I will hold the floor until the sentence ends.”

If the session goes off plan

If two panel members speak together, the chair immediately names the current owner and writes the other question in a deferred area. A deferred question is not a promise that extra time will be added. If an owner reaches for a third question, the chair says, “Your block has one follow-up; save the rest for another session.” If a question wanders into confidential or irrelevant territory, the candidate can decline and the owner substitutes a question within the agreed topic. If one panel member is absent, run a smaller conversation and label it as such; do not pretend that one person switching seats reproduces a panel. If the candidate needs a pause, stop questioning, then jointly decide whether to resume or end.

Debrief and constraints

The final conversation asks whether the candidate could identify the current question, whether the owners respected their boundaries, and which linking sentence would be worth practising next. It does not rank participants or produce an overall pass/fail verdict. The chair reads the topic map back and checks that a summary has not become a fabricated quote. The candidate chooses which notes to retain. This format cannot reproduce a real organisation’s panel dynamics, incentives, or decision process. Its useful output is a rehearsed handoff between topics and a clearer agreement about how peers will ask questions.
