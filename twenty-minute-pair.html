Peer interview session plans

A facilitator handbook for paper, a timer, and willing peers

Plan 1 of 5 · Two people; each practises one answer

20-minute pair rehearsal without an observer

Share the interviewer’s attention between listening and useful notes, while giving both people equal speaking time.

Run the timetableUse the session sheetGive bounded feedback

When to use this format

Use this plan when two peers have a short shared break and neither can recruit an observer. The constraint is attention: an interviewer cannot listen carefully, invent a question, and transcribe every sentence at once. The plan therefore limits questions and notes, separates the feedback turn from the interview turn, and gives each person the same seven-minute practice block. Success means leaving with one accurate observation and one small revision, not deciding who gave the better interview. Choose a question about a real, non-confidential experience that can be described without a long company introduction.

Roles, materials, and the opening cue

Call the participants A and B. A runs the timer during B’s answer; B runs it during A’s. Each speaker owns their facts and may decline a question. The interviewer asks the agreed opening question and at most two relevant follow-ups, then reflects back what they understood. There is no hidden assessor and no extra note-taking role. Put one sheet and a pen beside the timer. Write only a short phrase, a responsibility you understood, and one uncertainty. If you cannot confidently reproduce a phrase, label your note as a paraphrase rather than putting it in quotation marks.

At the start, A reads: “We will each practise once. Pick one thing you want the listener to understand. I will interrupt only for the agreed time signal or a boundary you set.” B reads the equivalent agreement back. Choose an unobtrusive stop word such as “time” and agree that a pause to protect private information is always allowed. Do not record by default. If either person wants recording, make a separate agreement before starting; otherwise keep this a paper-and-timer exercise. Each speaker writes a different opening question before the first answer so the second round does not depend on improvising a new topic.

The complete timetable

Time check: 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 20 minutes. Intervals use a running session clock, including transitions and debrief.

  1. 0–2 minutes · Agree the focus and boundaries

    Both name a question, a desired listener takeaway, and any topic to avoid. Facilitator cue: “What should I be able to explain back to you?”

  2. 2–5 minutes · A answers; B listens

    B reads A’s opening question once. A answers within the three-minute block. B notes only one phrase and one uncertainty, leaving attention for the answer.

  3. 5–7 minutes · B follows up

    Ask at most two questions about A’s actual answer. Cue: “Which part did you personally handle?” is available only if responsibility is unclear, not as an automatic script.

  4. 7–9 minutes · B gives feedback; A checks it

    B uses one minute to describe understanding and one uncertainty. A uses the remaining minute to confirm or correct the interpretation.

  5. 9–12 minutes · B answers; A listens

    Swap both speaking and timing responsibilities. A asks B’s prepared opening question and follows the same limited-note rule.

  6. 12–14 minutes · A follows up

    Ask no more than two questions tied to B’s words. Leave time for an answer; a second question is optional.

  7. 14–16 minutes · A gives feedback; B checks it

    A reports one observation. B distinguishes what they actually said from what the listener inferred.

  8. 16–18 minutes · Each retries one sentence

    A has one minute, then B has one minute. Repair only the sentence connected to the feedback; do not launch a second full story.

  9. 18–20 minutes · Debrief and close

    Each names one useful change and decides what to keep from the notes. Stop at twenty minutes rather than extending only the more talkative person’s turn.

A small fictional example

Fictional example: A describes reorganising a volunteer event check-in desk. B remembers that the queue moved but cannot identify A’s part. B’s follow-up asks which task A personally changed. A explains that they separated collection of preprinted badges from on-the-day registration. During the retry, A replaces “we fixed the desk” with a sentence naming that separation. No invented waiting-time percentage is added. B can now repeat the action, but neither person concludes that the change caused every improvement at the event.

Feedback prompts and a useful example

Ask three concrete questions: “What action did you understand me to take?”, “Where did you need information I had not supplied?”, and “Which sentence should I try again?” The interviewer answers from this attempt, not from everything they know about their friend. Bounded useful feedback sounds like: “I understood that you separated the two queues after your follow-up answer. In the first answer I heard only that the team fixed the desk. Put your action in the first explanation, then keep the team credit.” This is narrower than “be more confident” and does not claim knowledge of an employer’s judgement. If your only note is a remembered summary, introduce it as “My understanding was,” not “You said exactly.”

If the session goes off plan

If an answer reaches the time signal unfinished, let the speaker complete the current sentence, then use the follow-up block for one question about the missing point. Do not silently borrow the other person’s answer time. If a follow-up becomes advice, the speaker can say, “Please ask the question first; we can use the feedback minute for suggestions.” If both people know the project too well, the listener deliberately asks for one term a newcomer would need defined. If a sensitive name slips out, stop noting it, cross it out on paper, and restart with a role label. If either person declines the topic, use a less sensitive experience without demanding an explanation.

Debrief and constraints

Close by comparing the speaker’s intended takeaway with the listener’s actual takeaway, not by scoring delivery. Each person circles one sentence to practise later and crosses out one suggestion they will not use, with a short reason. Ask whether the timing felt balanced and whether taking fewer notes made listening easier. Keep only notes both people are comfortable retaining. This plan does not rehearse a long interview, measure fluency, or provide an independent account of everything said. The interviewer’s divided attention is a known limitation; use a triad another day if detailed observation is the priority.

Printable session sheet

Use paper, or type short notes into these temporary boxes and use your browser’s Print command. This page sends and stores no answers. Reloading or closing may discard typed notes.
